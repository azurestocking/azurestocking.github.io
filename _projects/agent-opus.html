Overview

Background

In Q2 2024, the company was a pre-Series A startup specialized in AI-powered video captioning and repurposing. Existing users were asking for text-to-video generation, a capability that naturally complements the current product, and the recent advances in generative AI had made this feature seem both timely and technically feasible.

This potentially valuable yet loosely defined opportunity needed to be validated and conceptualized quickly and affordably, before securing leadership buy-in and diverting core production efforts.

As the external consultant, I led rapid, iterative prototyping and testing to identify distinct user segments and their shared goals, shaping an end-to-end human-AI collaborative workflow across scriptwriting, storyboarding, and shot-level editing.

Challenge

I worked under low internal priority, so the core challenge was not just delivering the design, but resolving the strategic ambiguity beneath it.

Limited Resources

With Series A fundraising underway and a lean team stretched thin, there was no bandwidth for a full-time hire. Concept testing had to be fast and lightweight, leaving me with limited resources and visibility.

Twofold Ambiguity

The research needed to work on two fronts: strategically, to validate the opportunity by defining a value proposition and identifying target user segments; and tactically, to translate that into a feature design aligned with real user workflows.

Problem Statement

We began with a straightforward, feature-driven problem framing handed down by the product manager, simple by necessity, but knowingly incomplete:

How might we integrate a text-to-video generating feature into the existing short video repurposing pipeline?

As later research surfaced three high-level goals shared across two user segments, i.e., efficiency, consistency, and adaptability, I refined that rough question into something more specific:

How might we empower content creators to quickly and easily translate their scripts into reliably high-quality videos while accommodating diverse scenarios?

Here's how I got there.

Design Process

We adopted a Lean approach to move quickly and stay focused on user needs, using iterative prototyping to validate assumptions and evaluate outcomes, thereby enabling confident product direction without overcommitting resources.

The requests fell into two clusters along the locus axis, a dense one on the specify side and a sparser one on the delegate side, with usage running high among the specifiers and low among the delegators. Only one corner stayed almost empty, where a user hands a single, local decision to the system.

Three findings stood out:

User Segmentation

The requests didn't spread along a continuum; they clumped into two groups. One skew toward the high-intensity, specify-side users, such as indie filmmakers and animators; one toward the lower-intensity, delegate side, including influencers and content aggregators.

All-or-Nothing Delegation

Users who wanted the system to decide handed over entire projects, not individual choices, but those who retained control retained it at every scale. The dividing line was never how much automation users would accept, but whether a decision was theirs to make at all.

One Branching Pipeline

The locus tracked usage closely, while the scope cut evenly across both groups, implying that they needed the same range of capabilities but varying depths of control. This pointed away from two products and toward one pipeline with branching control depth.

Success Metric

Full implementation would be a major requiring leadership buy-in and broader organizational alignment, but none of which were in place yet. Therefore, I proactively defined success metrics to ensure the design's impact could be rigorously evaluated once the product was ready to ship:

At Launch

Evaluate initial user engagement and identify early signals of whether the design delivered value through the core workflow.

Activation Rate

Whether users completed their first storyboard, indicating the onboarding was intuitive enough to reach the key value moment.

Completion Rate

Whether users followed through the full pipeline from script to slideshow, reflecting end-to-end usability and perceived value.

Export Rate

Whether the rendered storyboard was compelling enough for users to act on — an early signal of output satisfaction.

Over Time

Assess sustained behavioral engagement and validate both the design strategy and its long-term business impact.

Feature Return Rate

Whether users consistently returned, indicating the workflow delivered sustained utility beyond initial curiosity.

Persona-Split Engagement

The ratio of user-specified to system-generated decisions per session, validating the two-segment strategy and informing future prioritization.

Trial-to-Paid Conversion Rate

Whether perceived value was strong enough to drive upgrade decisions, directly linking design quality to revenue.

The company raised a $30M Series A in August 2024 and a $20M Series B in March 2025. In September 2025, it launched a fully automated, standalone product narrowly tailored to faceless creators, rather than the product I had proposed.

The major differences in design and business strategy reflect a pivot toward a broader, lower-friction market amid intensifying competition, trading craft and control for speed, scale, and a more compelling investor narrative.

One year later, the company made a different bet, for reasons that were defensible at the time, and for one that became visible only afterward.

Competitive Repositioning

With startups like Runway and Pika earning early traction, and giants like Adobe and CapCut owning strong expertise, it is more prudent to strengthen existing revenue drivers than to compete in a vertical the company had no structural advantage in and no urgent need to win.

Narrative Reframing

Positioning as an autonomous agent rather than a smarter storyboard editor, and building a new suite rather than adding a tab, commanded a higher valuation multiple and aligned with where AI product discourse was heading in 2024-2025, as expected of a Series B company.

Hindsight

Does this mean the design failed?

No. Read against the papers that followed, it looks instead like an early practice of a decomposition the field adopted.

This design was built on the idea that workflow scaffolding could compensate for the base model's limitations. The workflow decomposition has proved correct; correct enough that the research community arrived at the same decomposition independently. What changed is where each piece lives.

Every row shares a concept but differs in method.

  • Our design provides GUI controls and review steps that users managed.
  • Agent systems automate parts of that workflow through coordinated LLM roles.
  • Model-level approaches use learned planning, explicit conditioning, and inference-time feature sharing.

Across these three implementations, a similar decomposition recurs, suggesting we broke the problem down in a useful way. What needed more thought was the division of labor: which parts users should direct, and which the system should handle.

🥪
Absorbed

The parts of scaffold that compensated for model limitations were absorbed first, and quickly. The intermediary stages, justified by compute cost and by base models "falling short" on sequencing, became redundant once keyframe planning was trained into the generator itself.

♻️
Remained

The parts of scaffold that captured users' decision were not absorbed, and those papers didn't try to. The two personas got further apart as models improved: delegators received one-pass generation; specifiers received models with more parameters to specify.

As generative models evolve this quickly, designers are faced with an uncomfortable yet inevitable question:

Where does design create lasting value?

In this case, collapsing the pipeline was an implicit wager that models would improve quickly enough to make intermediate checkpoints unnecessary. For the delegator persona, that wager paid off within a year, but disappeared controls from the interface did not make the underlying workflow obsolete. What it missed is that the checkpoints themselves were about to be adopted, by the models.

Redoing this in 2026, I would separate features into two kinds:

  • Model-limitation scaffolds built thin and expected to be absorbed within a model generation, such as entity enforcement, camera translation, the T2I preview, etc.
  • User-decision scaffolds built as the product, such as the entity list, shot intent, the review checkpoint, the iteration log, etc.

I would also make control depth elastic rather than staged: one representation the delegator sees as prompt-and-result and the specifier unfolds into shots and parameters.

Takeaway

Product decisions and priorities are rarely decided by design alone; it also needs to fit the company's business stage, earn the team's trust, and remain useful as the technology changes.

Stage-Driven Strategy

A pre-Series A startup validating a concept operates under fundamentally different constraints than a post-Series A company racing for market share — the same feature can warrant entirely different design directions depending on where a company stands in its growth cycle.

Trust-Building Method

Establishing credibility within the team early is what drives iterative direction and secures alignment. When direct peer support is absent, maintaining momentum means putting something tangible in front of stand-by colleagues to keep feedback flowing.

Design for What Lasts

Workflow design around a fast-moving model has to distinguish compensating for what the model cannot do today from capturing what the user wants regardless of the model. The first is a depreciating asset with a horizon of about one model generation; the second is the product. This design conflated them, which is why it looks superseded.
